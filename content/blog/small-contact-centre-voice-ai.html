Most of what's written about AI in contact centres is aimed at operations with hundreds or thousands of agents. If you run a team of 10 or 20, a lot of it doesn't apply. You don't have a transformation budget, a data science team or six months to run a pilot.

What you do have is a small team that gets stretched at the same points every week. That's where AI can help, and it's a much smaller, simpler job than the big case studies suggest.

The problem with being small

A small team has very little slack. One person off sick, a busy Monday morning or a problem that sends everyone to the phones at once, and queue times jump. You can't easily staff for the peaks, because you'd be paying people to sit idle the rest of the week.

Meanwhile, a good chunk of what your agents handle is the same handful of questions, asked over and over. Order status, appointment changes, opening times, "has my payment gone through?". Necessary, but not the best use of someone you trained for weeks.

Where a voice agent earns its keep

  • Peaks. An AI agent answers every call straight away, however many come in at once. It takes the routine calls, so your people are free for the ones that need them.
  • Out of hours. Evening and weekend callers get an answer, and often a resolution, instead of a voicemail you'll be clearing on Monday.
  • The repetitive stuff. Calls that follow a clear pattern and can be completed with a lookup in your systems are the best fit. Booking, rescheduling, status checks, simple account changes.
  • Taking the details first. Even when a call needs a person, the AI can identify the caller and find out what they need, then pass all of that across. Your agent picks up knowing what the call is about.

Where it doesn't

It's worth being just as clear about this.

  • Complaints and upset customers. Someone who's already frustrated wants a person. Route them to one quickly.
  • Complex, one-off problems. If your agents have to investigate, use judgement or negotiate, keep those calls with them.
  • Processes that only live in people's heads. If nobody has written down how a task is done, an AI can't do it either. Sometimes the useful first step is simply documenting the process.

Start small

You don't need to automate everything at once. The approach that works for small teams is to pick one or two call types that are high in volume and simple to complete, put the AI on those, and watch how it goes. Keep a clear route to a person on every call. Expand once you've seen it work with your own customers.

Before you speak to any vendor, it's worth pulling a month of call data from your phone system: volumes by hour, and a rough split of what people are calling about. That tells you very quickly whether there's a real case, and which calls to start with.

A word on cost

Most voice AI is priced on usage, by the minute or by the conversation, plus a setup cost. For a small team, that can work well, because you pay for what's handled rather than for seats. But look at the whole picture: setup, connecting to your systems, ongoing changes and support, not just the per-minute rate.
