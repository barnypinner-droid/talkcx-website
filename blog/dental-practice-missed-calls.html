Where do your practice's missed calls actually go?

← All posts

23 November 2026 · Barny Pinner

Ask any practice manager about the phones and you'll hear the same thing. Monday mornings are chaos, lunchtimes are worse, and the moment a patient is standing at the desk, the phone starts ringing.

Most practices know the phones get busy. Far fewer know what happens to the calls nobody picks up. That's the bit worth looking at, because those calls don't just disappear.

Where a missed call goes

When a patient rings and doesn't get through, they do one of a few things:

  • They ring back later. Fine, but it adds to the next busy spell.
  • They leave a voicemail. Someone now has to listen to it, call back, and often play phone tag.
  • They give up for now. The check-up gets pushed back, or forgotten.
  • They ring the practice down the road. For a new patient, this is usually what happens.

Existing patients tend to be patient. New patients, and anyone in pain, aren't. They're calling the next number on the list, and the first practice to answer gets the booking.

Find out your own number first

Before you spend anything, get a rough picture of what's happening now. Your phone system almost certainly records it. Ask whoever supplies it for a report showing, for the last four weeks:

  1. Total inbound calls
  2. Calls answered, and calls abandoned or sent to voicemail
  3. The same, broken down by hour of the day

The hourly view is the useful one. In most practices the missed calls bunch up in a few predictable windows: first thing, lunchtime, and the end of the day. That tells you whether you have a staffing problem across the whole day, or a capacity problem for a few hours a week.

What the calls are actually about

The next question is what people are ringing for. Sit with reception for a morning, or ask them to tally calls for a week. You'll usually find a big share are routine:

  • Booking, moving or cancelling an appointment
  • Opening hours, parking, directions
  • "Are you taking new NHS patients?"
  • Prices for common treatments

These are important calls, but they don't need your most experienced person. The calls that do need a human are the anxious patient, the complicated treatment question, the complaint.

Your options

There's no single right answer. The realistic choices are:

  • Change the rota. If the misses are concentrated in two or three windows, an extra pair of hands at those times may fix most of it.
  • Online booking. Helpful for patients who'd rather not call, though plenty still pick up the phone.
  • An outsourced answering service. Someone answers, but usually takes a message rather than booking anything.
  • An AI voice agent. It answers every call immediately, handles the routine ones end to end, and passes anything that needs a person to your team with the details already taken.

On that last one, a fair word of caution. A good voice agent sounds natural, understands accents and people changing their mind mid-sentence, and knows when to hand over. A poor one is a phone menu with a nicer voice, and patients can tell. If you look at this route, ask to hear it handle a real, messy call, not a scripted demo.

Where to start

Pull the call report. If the missed calls are a handful a week, you probably don't need us. If they're a steady stream at the times you're busiest, it's worth a proper look at what they're costing you and what would fix it.

Either way, you'll know, rather than guess. And if you'd like a second pair of eyes on the numbers, we're happy to help.
